  1. Don't Be a Snitch: when do LLM agents hide rule-breaking from oversight?In a team of coding agents, a worker learns that a teammate broke a rule. Does it tell? Across 1,320 sessions, a peer's request to keep quiet worked only when the act looked defensible, while the lead's worked at every place tested, and on three more models.
    • Built a test bed for cover-ups in agent teams: two code repositories with written rules and hidden checks, a lead and two workers acting only through logged tools, and scripted teammates as controls. The outcome is measured by branching each finished session and asking the user's question on its own copy, so asking cannot change the session.
    • On GPT-6 Luna, a peer's request to hide the act from the lead worked only when the act looked defensible but flagged (22 vs. 38 of 40 answers named it); on a clear breach or a security gap it made workers report as often or more. The same request from the lead worked at all three places tested (0, 2 and 3 of 40 answers named the act), with no pushback.
    • A model lead asked to hide an edit never agreed (0 of 160), but its report named the edit only while it was still in the repository, so a revert kept it from the user.
    • The cover-ups were omissions, not lies: asked directly, 149 of 151 silent workers admitted the act. Oversight should ask pointed questions, compare reports with the repository's history, and log every request for silence.
    • On Qwen3.8-27B, MiniMax M3.1 Flash and Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite the lead's request again worked (4, 14 and 0 of 20 answers named the act, against 20, 20 and 19 when told only that the lead knew), while a peer's mostly failed.

  2. Steering language models: manifold and concept steeringInterpretability research on how concepts live inside language models, and techniques to steer them.
    • Challenged Goodfire's state-of-the-art manifold steering claims by designing a simple vector steering method that reduces correct-output probability by at most 1%.
    • Proved that same-concept manifolds across different languages collapse into one shared structure as models scale in size.

    github.com/neilshah13/causalab

  3. Learning to control an exact solverAn RL-learned branching heuristic for exact, #P-hard chromatic polynomials. 19% smaller search trees than the standard heuristic, verified against a brute-force oracle with zero mismatches across all 328 instances tested.
    • Built a C++ deletion-contraction engine (exact GMP arithmetic, a nauty isomorphism cache, and clique-separator decomposition) and trained an RL policy to choose the branching edge: 19% reduction in search trees from the standard heuristic.
    • Derived graph instances from Natural Earth geodata (adjacency as geodesic shared-border length) and computed the exact chromatic polynomial of the world map (195 UN-recognized entities, χ = 4, 34,703 branches) plus all 214 countries' internal subdivisions, served through an interactive recursion-tree visualiser.
